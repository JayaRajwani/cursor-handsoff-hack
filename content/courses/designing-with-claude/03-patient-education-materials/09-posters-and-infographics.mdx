Posters and infographics communicate at a glance. A waiting-room poster may have three seconds of attention; a conference poster perhaps two minutes. Claude can help you decide **what to say**, **how to structure it** and even **build a first visual version**.

## Three common types of healthcare poster

| Type | Goal | Typical length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Public health / waiting room** | One behaviour change (e.g. "Clean your hands") | Under 40 words |
| **Staff information** | A process or reminder (e.g. sepsis screening steps) | 50–150 words |
| **Academic / conference** | Present research or a quality project | 400–800 words |

## The visual hierarchy

Good posters are read in a predictable order: **headline → image or key visual → key message → supporting detail → call to action**. Ask Claude to write for that order:

<Prompt title="Waiting-room poster">

Write the text for an A3 waiting-room poster encouraging patients to tell staff about all the medicines they take, including herbal remedies. Provide: a headline of no more than six words, a subheading of no more than 15 words, three short bullet points, and a call to action. Suggest a simple, culturally appropriate image idea that does not show any identifiable people.

</Prompt>

## Building a visual draft with artifacts

Claude can create a simple visual design — for example as an HTML page or an SVG graphic — in the artifact panel. This is ideal for quick drafts and infographics.

<Prompt title="Infographic draft">

Create an infographic as an SVG artifact titled "Five moments for medication safety". Use a vertical layout with five numbered steps, a simple line icon for each, our colours (navy #0B2545, teal #0F766E, white background) and large text. Keep it accessible: high contrast and no information conveyed by colour alone.

</Prompt>

You can then ask for changes ("make step 3 more prominent", "use a horizontal layout"), download the file, and refine it further in your design software if needed.

<Callout type="note" title="Images and copyright">

Do not copy images, logos or icons from the internet without permission. Use images your organisation owns, licensed stock images, or simple graphics created for you. Never use photographs of patients without documented consent and approval.

</Callout>

## Academic posters

For conference posters, Claude is especially helpful at **condensing** — turning a 3,000-word report into concise poster sections — and suggesting which result to show visually. Remember:

- Check every number against your original data; never let AI "round" or restate results without checking.
- Follow the conference's size, layout and disclosure rules.
- Many journals and conferences require disclosure of AI assistance in writing; check their policy.

## Design checklist for posters

- One clear key message, visible from two metres away
- Headline font large enough to read across a room
- No more than three colours, used consistently
- Logos only where you have permission
- Contact details or QR code for more information
- Accessible contrast and plain language

<Exercise title="Design a poster">

Create a waiting-room or staff poster on a topic relevant to your work. Use Claude to write the text, then ask it to produce a visual draft as an artifact. Note two changes you would make before printing.

</Exercise>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Match the length and structure to the poster type and viewing time.
- Write for the visual hierarchy: headline, visual, key message, detail, action.
- Artifacts give quick visual drafts; always respect copyright, consent and conference rules.

</KeyTakeaways>
