Before we design anything, we need a clear set of safety rules. These apply to every lesson in this course and to every AI tool you will ever use at work.

## Rule 1: Never enter identifiable patient information

Information that could identify a patient must never be typed, pasted or uploaded into a general-purpose AI tool unless your organisation has explicitly approved that specific tool for that purpose. Identifiable information includes much more than a name:

- Names, initials combined with other details, or nicknames
- Hospital, medical record (MR) or national ID numbers (for example CNIC numbers)
- Dates of birth, admission or discharge dates
- Addresses, postcodes, phone numbers and email addresses
- Photographs, scans or documents showing faces, labels or identifiers
- Rare conditions, unusual occupations or local events that could identify someone in context

<Callout type="privacy" title="The newspaper test">

Before you paste anything into an AI tool, ask: *"If this text appeared in a newspaper, could anyone — including the patient's family or neighbours — work out who it is about?"* If the answer is yes or maybe, do not paste it.

</Callout>

### How to de-identify an example

For this course you almost never need real patient details. Instead of de-identifying real cases, it is safer to **invent a representative example**:

| Instead of… | Use… |
| --- | --- |
| "Mrs Ayesha K., 67, MR 445812, admitted 3 March with…" | "A woman in her late 60s admitted with…" |
| A photo of a real wound | A description, or a licensed teaching image |
| The actual discharge letter | A template with the structure but no patient data |

## Rule 2: Verify every clinical fact

Claude can produce errors that look authoritative. Every drug name, dose, threshold, statistic, recommendation and reference in an AI draft must be checked against a trusted, current source — national or international guidelines, your local formulary, product information or your institution's protocols.

A practical approach is to ask Claude to **mark claims that need verification** and then check each one yourself:

<Prompt title="Ask Claude to flag what needs checking">

At the end of the leaflet, add a section called "For clinical reviewer" that lists every factual clinical claim in the text (doses, timings, thresholds, statistics) as a numbered checklist so I can verify each one against our local guideline.

</Prompt>

## Rule 3: Watch for bias and exclusion

AI models learn from existing text, which contains biases. Drafts may assume a particular culture, diet, literacy level, gender or family structure. Review materials for:

- Examples and images that reflect your actual patient population
- Dietary and lifestyle advice that is culturally appropriate (for example, for patients who fast during Ramadan)
- Language that is respectful, non-judgemental and inclusive
- Accessibility: large fonts, good contrast, simple words, and alternatives for people who cannot read

## Rule 4: Follow your institution's policy — and be transparent

- Find out whether your organisation has an **AI use policy** and which tools are approved.
- Use AI-assisted materials only after the normal **review and approval** process (for example a patient information committee).
- Be open about AI assistance when appropriate. Many organisations ask staff to note "Drafted with AI assistance and reviewed by [role]" on internal documents.
- Keep a simple **record** of what you used AI for and who reviewed the output.

## Rule 5: You are accountable

Professional regulators are clear that clinicians remain responsible for the information they give patients and colleagues, whatever tool helped produce it. AI is a drafting assistant; the professional judgement is yours.

<Callout type="warning" title="When not to use AI at all">

Do not use general AI tools for urgent clinical decisions, for interpreting individual patient results, or for anything you cannot verify. If in doubt, ask your supervisor or information governance lead.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Never enter identifiable patient information into AI tools — invent representative examples instead.
- Verify every clinical fact against trusted, current sources.
- Review drafts for bias and accessibility, follow your institution's policy, and remember that you remain accountable.

</KeyTakeaways>
