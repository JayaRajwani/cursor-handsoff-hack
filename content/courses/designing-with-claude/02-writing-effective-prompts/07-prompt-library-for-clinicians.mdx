Once you find a prompt that works well, save it. A **prompt library** is simply a document of tested prompts you can reuse and adapt. It saves time, produces consistent results and is easy to share with your team.

## How to organise a prompt library

Keep it simple — a single document with headings works well:

1. **Patient information** — leaflets, discharge advice, FAQs, appointment letters
2. **Teaching** — lesson plans, slide outlines, case scenarios, quizzes
3. **Presentations and posters** — talk outlines, abstract-to-poster, speaker notes
4. **Admin and communication** — emails, meeting summaries, newsletters
5. **Review and quality** — accuracy checklists, tone checks, readability checks

For each prompt record: what it is for, the prompt itself (with square-bracket placeholders), and any tips from using it.

## Five starter prompts

<Prompt title="1. Patient FAQ from a guideline">

Using only the attached (non-confidential) guideline, write 10 frequently asked questions and answers for patients about [topic]. Use plain language at a reading age of about 11. If the guideline does not answer a question, write "Ask your healthcare team" rather than guessing.

</Prompt>

<Prompt title="2. Teaching session plan">

Create a 45-minute teaching session plan for [audience] on [topic]. Include learning objectives, a 5-minute icebreaker, three main sections with timings, one interactive activity, and a 5-question end-of-session quiz with answers.

</Prompt>

<Prompt title="3. Abstract to poster">

Here is my research abstract. Turn it into the text for an A0 academic poster with these sections: title, background, methods, results, conclusions, and a "key message" box of no more than 20 words. Keep the total under 600 words and suggest which result should be shown as a chart.

</Prompt>

<Prompt title="4. Professional email">

Draft a polite, concise email to [recipient role] about [topic]. The purpose is to [goal]. Keep it under 150 words and end with a clear request.

</Prompt>

<Prompt title="5. Accuracy review">

Act as a careful clinical reviewer. List every factual claim in this document in a table with three columns: claim, type (dose, statistic, recommendation, other), and "needs checking against". Do not judge whether the claims are correct.

</Prompt>

## Share and improve as a team

A shared prompt library is a simple, powerful way to spread good practice in a department. Agree on a place to store it (for example your team's shared drive), review it every few months, and note who maintains it. Make sure it includes your safety reminders — especially the rule about patient information.

<Callout type="privacy" title="Keep templates free of patient data">

Prompt templates should contain placeholders, never real examples copied from patient records.

</Callout>
