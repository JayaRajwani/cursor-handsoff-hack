Many patients struggle to understand written health information. Studies across many countries show that a large proportion of adults have limited health literacy, and stress, illness and language barriers make it harder still. Plain language is not "dumbing down" — it is good clinical communication.

## The principles of plain language

- **Short sentences** — aim for 15–20 words on average.
- **Everyday words** — "high blood pressure" rather than "hypertension"; "kidney" rather than "renal".
- **Active voice** — "Take one tablet each morning" rather than "One tablet should be taken".
- **Speak to the reader** — use "you" and "we".
- **One idea per paragraph**, with clear headings that answer the reader's questions.
- **Explain necessary medical terms** the first time you use them.
- **Tell people what to do**, not just what to know.

## Prompts for plain-language rewriting

<Prompt title="Simplify a technical paragraph">

Rewrite the following paragraph for patients with limited health literacy. Use a reading age of about 10–11, short sentences and everyday words. Keep every clinical fact the same — do not add or remove medical information. Then list any words you replaced and what you replaced them with.

[Paste a non-confidential technical paragraph here]

</Prompt>

The final instruction — list the words you replaced — makes it easy for you to check that meaning has not changed.

<Prompt title="Answer the patient's real questions">

Here is the outline of a leaflet about having a colonoscopy. List the ten questions a worried first-time patient is most likely to have, and check whether the outline answers each one. Suggest additions for any that are missing.

</Prompt>

## Tone: kind, calm and respectful

Patients reading leaflets are often anxious. Ask Claude for a tone that is warm but not patronising, and avoid language that blames ("non-compliant", "failed to") or frightens unnecessarily. You can ask:

<Prompt title="Check the tone">

Review this leaflet for tone. Highlight any sentences that might sound blaming, frightening, patronising or overly technical to a patient, and suggest a kinder alternative for each.

</Prompt>

## Readability scores — useful but limited

Readability formulas (such as Flesch Reading Ease) estimate difficulty from sentence and word length. Claude can estimate these, but its numbers are approximations — use a dedicated readability checker if you need an exact score. More importantly, a good score does not guarantee understanding. The best test is to **ask real patients or lay colleagues** to read the draft and explain it back to you.

## Translation and multilingual materials

Claude can help draft translations (for example into Urdu or other regional languages), but **machine-translated health information must be checked by a qualified, fluent speaker** — ideally a professional medical translator — before use. Small errors in translation can change clinical meaning. Also check that the script renders correctly in your design software and on printed copies.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not change the clinical meaning">

When simplifying, Claude occasionally drops an important caveat or slightly changes a recommendation. Always compare the simplified version with the original, line by line, before approving it.

</Callout>

<Exercise title="Simplify and compare">

Find a paragraph of technical text you would normally give to patients (from a non-confidential source, such as a public guideline summary). Ask Claude to simplify it using the prompt above. Compare the two versions and note any changes in meaning.

</Exercise>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Plain language is good clinical communication: short sentences, everyday words, clear actions.
- Ask Claude to preserve every clinical fact and to list the changes it made.
- Readability scores help, but testing with real readers is better; translations need expert review.

</KeyTakeaways>
