Training material is where AI can save the most time. Induction packs, competency workbooks, case-based teaching, quizzes and simulation scripts all follow recognisable structures that Claude handles well — provided you supply the clinical content and check the result.

## Start with learning objectives

Good training starts with what learners should be able to **do** afterwards. Ask Claude to help write objectives using action verbs:

<Prompt title="Learning objectives">

Write four measurable learning objectives for a 30-minute session for newly qualified nurses on recognising and escalating the deteriorating patient. Use action verbs (for example identify, calculate, demonstrate, escalate) and make each objective assessable.

</Prompt>

## Case scenarios

Fictional case scenarios make teaching memorable. Claude can create realistic, varied scenarios quickly — and because they are invented, there is no privacy risk.

<Prompt title="Case scenarios">

Create three fictional, realistic case scenarios for teaching junior doctors about safe prescribing in older adults. Each scenario should include a brief history, current medicines, one hidden prescribing problem, two discussion questions and a model answer. Use invented names and details that cannot be linked to real patients. Make the scenarios culturally relevant to a hospital in South Asia.

</Prompt>

Always ask a subject expert to check that the clinical details and model answers are correct.

## Quizzes and knowledge checks

<Prompt title="Multiple-choice quiz">

Using only the attached (non-confidential) policy, write 10 multiple-choice questions to check staff understanding. Each question should have four options with one correct answer, plausible distractors, and a one-sentence explanation of the correct answer that cites the relevant policy section.

</Prompt>

Tips: mix recall questions with application questions ("What should you do next?"); avoid trick questions; and review every answer key yourself.

## Simulation and role-play scripts

Claude can draft scripts for communication skills training — breaking bad news, handling complaints, talking to an anxious relative — including a facilitator guide and debrief questions. You can even ask Claude to **play the role** of a fictional patient or relative so learners can practise.

<Prompt title="Role-play practice">

Act as a worried adult son whose fictional father has been admitted with a fall. You are frustrated because nobody has updated you. I am the ward nurse. Respond realistically, one turn at a time, and do not resolve too quickly. When I type "debrief", stop the role-play and give me feedback on my communication using the structure: what went well, what to improve, suggested phrases.

</Prompt>

## Induction and orientation packs

For an induction pack, give Claude the structure and non-confidential facts (department layout, key contacts by role, routines) and ask it to produce a friendly, well-organised document with a checklist for the first week. Add your organisation's mandatory content and policies yourself.

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep a single source of truth">

Training material goes out of date when policies change. Link to the official policy rather than copying large sections, include a version and review date, and name an owner responsible for updates.

</Callout>

<Exercise title="Create a mini training session">

Build a 20-minute training session on a topic you teach: three learning objectives, one fictional case scenario and a five-question quiz. Ask a colleague to review the clinical content.

</Exercise>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Start with measurable learning objectives.
- Use invented case scenarios, quizzes and role-plays — they are fast to create and privacy-safe.
- Experts must check clinical content and answer keys; keep material versioned and owned.

</KeyTakeaways>
