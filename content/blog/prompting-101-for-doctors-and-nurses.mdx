If you have tried an AI assistant and been disappointed by generic, waffly or inaccurate answers, the problem is often the prompt. AI assistants respond to what you ask; the more clearly you ask, the better the result. This guide introduces a simple framework that healthcare professionals can use straight away.

## Why prompts matter so much

An AI assistant knows nothing about your situation unless you tell it. "Write a leaflet about asthma" could produce something for children or adults, for the UK or Pakistan, at a reading age of 8 or 18. Each missing detail is filled with a guess — and guesses add editing time and risk.

## The CLEAR framework

We teach a five-part framework called **CLEAR**:

- **C — Context:** Who you are, where you work and what the material is for.
- **L — Listener:** Who will read it and what they already know.
- **E — Expected output:** Format, length, structure and tone.
- **A — Accuracy guardrails:** Sources to use, things to exclude, and a request to flag uncertainty.
- **R — Review and refine:** Treat the answer as a draft and improve it with specific feedback.

## Before and after

**Before:**

> Make a presentation about sepsis.

**After:**

> I am an emergency medicine registrar teaching final-year medical students. Create a slide-by-slide outline for a 20-minute teaching session on recognising sepsis in the emergency department. Maximum 12 slides. Write each slide title as a key message, with no more than three bullet points per slide and a suggested visual. Base the clinical content on the attached (non-confidential) local sepsis pathway only, and finish with three case-based questions for discussion. Flag any statement you are not certain is in the pathway.

The second prompt takes a minute longer to write, but the output is immediately usable.

## Three habits that improve every prompt

1. **Invite questions.** End with: *"Before you start, ask me up to three questions if anything important is unclear."*
2. **Provide a source.** Upload or paste a non-confidential guideline or policy and tell the AI to use only that.
3. **Ask for a verification list.** *"At the end, list every clinical claim so I can check it."*

## Follow-up prompts that save time

- "Give me three alternative versions: formal, friendly and very concise."
- "Rewrite this for patients at a reading age of about 11, keeping every clinical fact the same."
- "Critique your draft against my requirements and fix any gaps."
- "Turn this into a table."

## What not to do

- Do not paste identifiable patient information — ever.
- Do not accept doses, statistics or references without checking them.
- Do not use AI output as a clinical decision. It is a drafting assistant.

## Start practising

The best way to learn prompting is to practise on real, low-risk tasks: rewriting a leaflet you already have, outlining your next teaching session, or drafting an email. Keep the prompts that work in a personal "prompt library".
