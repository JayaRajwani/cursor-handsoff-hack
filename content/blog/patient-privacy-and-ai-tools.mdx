AI assistants are now available on every phone and laptop, and many healthcare workers are already using them — sometimes without realising the privacy risks. This article explains, in plain language, what you need to know to use AI tools without putting patients, your organisation or your registration at risk.

*This article is general education, not legal advice. Always follow your institution's policies and the laws that apply where you work.*

## Why AI tools are different from your hospital system

When you type into a general-purpose AI tool, your text is sent to a company's servers to be processed. Depending on the service, your plan and your settings, conversations may be stored, reviewed for safety, or used to improve future models. Even when a provider offers strong privacy protections, sharing patient information with a service that your organisation has not approved may breach confidentiality rules and data-protection law.

## What counts as identifiable information?

Most people know not to type a patient's name. But identification is broader than names. Treat all of the following as identifiable:

- Names, initials and nicknames
- Medical record, hospital and national identity numbers
- Dates of birth, admission, discharge or death
- Addresses, phone numbers, email addresses
- Photographs, scans or screenshots containing faces, labels or identifiers
- Combinations of details — a rare condition, an unusual job and a small town may identify someone even without a name

A useful test: *if this text were published in a newspaper, could anyone work out who it is about?* If yes — or maybe — do not enter it.

## "But I removed the name"

Removing a name is not the same as anonymisation. Clinical narratives often contain enough detail to re-identify a person, especially in smaller communities. For education and communication tasks, the safest approach is not to de-identify real cases at all, but to **invent representative examples**. AI tools are very good at generating realistic fictional scenarios for teaching.

## Five rules for safe use

1. **Use only approved tools for work.** Ask your IT or information governance team which tools are permitted, and use organisational accounts where provided.
2. **Never enter identifiable patient or staff information** into a tool that is not approved for that purpose.
3. **Check your privacy settings**, including whether your conversations may be used for model training.
4. **Verify everything.** AI can be confidently wrong; you remain accountable for what you produce.
5. **Be transparent.** Follow your organisation's rules on recording or disclosing AI assistance.

## What you *can* safely do

There is plenty of valuable, low-risk work AI can help with:

- Drafting patient leaflets from public, non-confidential guidelines
- Simplifying the language of materials you have written
- Creating fictional case scenarios, quizzes and teaching plans
- Structuring presentations, posters and reports from aggregated, non-identifiable data
- Drafting emails, agendas and summaries that contain no confidential information

## If you make a mistake

If you realise you have entered identifiable information into an AI tool, do not ignore it. Delete the conversation where possible, and report it promptly through your organisation's information governance or incident reporting process. Early reporting allows the organisation to manage the risk properly.

## The bottom line

AI can genuinely improve healthcare communication and education. The professionals who benefit most are those who combine curiosity with discipline: they use AI enthusiastically for the right tasks, and never compromise patient confidentiality.
